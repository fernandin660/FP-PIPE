Every supplier eventually has a bad day. What customers remember is how you handled it, and last week gave a very public example.

## The case

On Thursday 12 November, business users around the world lost access to Outlook and Teams. [ABC News](https://abcnews.com/US/outlook-down-microsoft-outage-reported-mail/story?id=138088203) reported that Downdetector logged more than 100,000 reports within an hour of Microsoft acknowledging the problem, and the disruption spanned two working days. Some users were without their mailbox for more than 18 hours, according to [Variety](https://variety.com/2026/digital/news/microsoft-apologizes-outlook-outage-customer-credits-1236947124/), with service returning region by region from Friday.

Microsoft said the cause was an issue during an earlier network capacity upgrade that surfaced after a later network change, and that it was not a cyberattack. On Friday the company apologised publicly, saying it was "deeply sorry for the disruption", and promised a full post-incident report. It also acknowledged that the gradual recovery had taken longer than hoped because of the scale of demand, as [FOX 2](https://www.fox2detroit.com/news/microsoft-apologizes-widespread-outage-what-went-wrong-deeply-sorry) reported.

One detail matters for anyone who sells to businesses. Variety reported that affected business customers may be entitled to service credits, but these are not applied automatically: the customer has to submit a request. Whatever you think of that policy, it's a useful prompt to look at how your own company behaves when something goes wrong.

## Lesson 1: tell customers before they have to ask

During an incident, silence is filled with guesses. A short, honest message early, saying what you know, what you don't, and when you'll update next, buys far more patience than a perfect explanation two days later.

Prepare this before you need it. Write a simple template for "something is wrong", agree who sends it, and keep an up-to-date list of the main contact at each account. A logistics company that warns its customers about a delayed shipment at 9am gets a very different reaction from one that waits for the angry call at 4pm.

## Lesson 2: own the apology, then show the fix

Microsoft's statement did three things: it apologised, it explained the cause in plain terms, and it committed to a follow-up report. That structure works at any scale.

When you're the one apologising to a client, avoid hiding behind "a third-party issue" or "unforeseen circumstances". Say what happened, what you're changing so it doesn't repeat, and when you'll confirm the change is in place. Then actually come back with that confirmation. That second contact is where trust is rebuilt.

## Lesson 3: make compensation easy to receive

If your company offers credits, discounts or extra service after a failure, think about who has to do the work to get them. Asking an already frustrated customer to fill in a form can turn a goodwill gesture into another irritation.

For your largest accounts, consider handling it for them: "We've applied a credit to your next invoice for the downtime". Even if the amount is small, the message is that you noticed and acted without being chased.

## Lesson 4: protect the renewal, starting this week

Incidents rarely lose a customer on the day. They lose them months later, when the contract is up and someone in the buying committee remembers "the time everything stopped". If you sell into accounts that were affected by a problem, yours or a supplier's, flag them in your CRM and schedule a check-in within a couple of weeks.

Use that conversation to listen rather than to sell. Ask what the disruption cost them, what would have helped, and whether anything else is worrying them. You'll often hear about needs you didn't know existed.

## Lesson 5: in prospecting, preparedness is a selling point

Outages like this one remind buyers that reliability and support matter as much as features. When you're talking to new prospects, don't wait for them to raise it. Explain how you communicate during incidents, who they can call, and how quickly you respond, with a real example if you have one.

A payroll provider, for instance, can win a deal not by having the longest feature list but by showing exactly what happens if a payment run fails at midnight.

## Your move

Pick your ten most important accounts. If something went wrong tomorrow, would you know who to call at each one, and what you'd say in the first hour? If not, that list and a two-line template are your homework for this week.
