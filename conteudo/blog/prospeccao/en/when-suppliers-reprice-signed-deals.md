A signed contract usually feels like the end of the price conversation. This week's reports from the AI server market show that, when costs move fast enough, it can become the start of a much harder one, and how a seller handles that moment decides whether the account survives it.

## The case

According to reporting first published by the Financial Times on 22 November and summarised by [Tom's Hardware](https://www.tomshardware.com/tech-industry/big-tech/ai-server-makers-hike-prices-up-to-45-percent-months-after-contracts-are-signed-as-ram-shortage-triples-memory-heavy-cpu-node-costs-financial-firms-still-pay-up-as-the-compute-is-valuable) and [TrendForce](https://www.trendforce.com/news/2026/11/23/news-ai-server-prices-reportedly-rise-up-to-45-as-dell-hpe-and-others-raise-prices-after-deals/), server makers including Dell, HPE, Lenovo and Supermicro have raised prices for some customers months after contracts were signed. The rises reach up to 45% in some cases, according to unnamed sources. The main driver is memory: prices for GPU servers are reported to be up roughly 20% to 30%, while nodes heavy in CPUs and memory have doubled or more.

The buyers mentioned are mostly trading and financial firms. One of them, XTX Markets, is in a legal dispute with Dell's Irish unit over a server contract; both outlets report that XTX had paid extra for price certainty before Dell sought a higher amount. The case is ongoing and the claims have not been decided by a court. TrendForce also reports that HPE changed its quoting terms so it can reprice existing orders if component costs rise before shipment.

Many of these buyers are paying anyway. One unnamed buyer quoted in the coverage explained why: "the compute is valuable to us". That tells you something about supplier power in a shortage, but it doesn't mean the relationship comes out unharmed.

You may never sell servers, but cost shocks happen in every industry: steel, freight, packaging, energy, software licences. Here is what sales teams can take from this.

## Talk about price risk before the contract, not after

Most repricing fights start because the risk was never discussed openly. If your costs depend on something volatile, say so during the negotiation. Explain which parts of the price are fixed, which could move, and under what conditions. A buyer who hears this upfront may push back, but they're making an informed decision. A buyer who discovers it after signing feels misled, whatever the contract says.

For example, a packaging supplier quoting a 12-month deal can offer two options: a fixed price with a premium built in, or a lower base price with an agreed index for raw material. Letting the customer choose turns a hidden risk into a shared decision.

## Make "firm price" mean something

The coverage shows a buyer reportedly paying extra for certainty and still facing a dispute. Whatever the outcome of that case, it shows how much weight buyers place on the words "firm" and "fixed". If your company can't absorb a cost spike, don't sell certainty you can't deliver. If you do sell it, make sure finance, operations and legal agree before the proposal goes out. A promise made by sales alone is the one most likely to be broken.

## Deliver bad news early, in person, with options

If you do have to pass on a cost increase, the way you do it matters as much as the number. Call before you send the letter. Show what changed, with evidence the buyer can check. Then offer choices: a phased increase, a smaller order, a later delivery date, a different specification, or a longer commitment in exchange for a better rate. A customer who is given options feels like a partner. A customer who receives a new invoice feels like a hostage.

## Supplier power doesn't last forever

In a shortage, buyers often pay because they have no alternative. Sellers can mistake that for loyalty. When supply loosens, customers remember who treated them fairly and who used the moment to squeeze. Track which accounts accepted increases reluctantly and plan how you'll rebuild goodwill: priority on delivery, extra support, or a review of terms when costs fall. Accounts are rarely lost on the day of the price rise; they're lost at the next tender.

## Expect buyers to ask harder questions

After stories like this, procurement teams will ask about repricing clauses, cost indexes and price protection more often. Prepare clear, honest answers in advance, and train your team to explain them simply. Being the supplier who answers these questions plainly is a competitive advantage, especially against rivals who dodge them.

## Your move

Look at the contracts you'll sign in the next quarter. For each one, can you say which costs could move, who carries that risk, and how you'd tell the customer if they did? If the answer is unclear, sort it out before the signature, not after.
