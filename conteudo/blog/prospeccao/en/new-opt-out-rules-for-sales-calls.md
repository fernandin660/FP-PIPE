A prospect who replies "stop" is giving you information, not just a complaint. From this week, US rules on how that information must be handled are changing, and sales teams that call or text should check their process before the deadline.

## What is changing

The Federal Communications Commission revised its rules under the Telephone Consumer Protection Act (TCPA), the US law that governs many automated and prerecorded calls and texts. According to a client alert from [Pierce Atwood](https://www.pierceatwood.com/alerts/fcc-revises-tcpa-opt-out-rules-changes-take-effect-november-25-2026) published on November 17, and analyses by [Wiley](https://www.wiley.law/alert-FCC-Revises-TCPA-Rules-for-Informational-Calls-Seeks-Comment-on-Further-Changes) and [Legal500](https://www.legal500.com/intelligence/united-states/consumer-protection/order-out-of-chaos-fccs-new-tcpa-consent-revocation-rules-go-into-effect-on-november-25-2026), the changes take effect on November 25, 2026.

Three points stand out for sales teams. First, a company may now designate one method people must use to opt out, such as a keypress option, standard text replies, or a specific website or phone number, as long as it "clearly and conspicuously discloses" that method. Second, text systems must still recognize common keywords such as stop, quit, end, revoke, opt out, cancel and unsubscribe. Third, an opt-out from one telemarketing message ends consent for all future telemarketing from that sender, while opt-outs from informational messages, such as payment reminders, can be limited to that type. Opt-outs must be honored within 10 business days, and the FCC is consulting on shortening that window.

The law firms also note that stricter state laws still apply. None of the sources spells out how every rule applies to business-to-business calls, so treat this as a prompt to talk to your legal adviser, not as legal advice. Still, there are practical sales lessons regardless of how your situation is classified.

## Know exactly how you reach people

Many teams don't have a clear picture of their own outreach. One rep uses a dialer with prerecorded voicemail drops, another sends texts from a sequencing tool, and marketing runs SMS campaigns from a third platform. Rules like these apply to specific technologies and message types, so you can't assess your exposure without a map.

List every tool that places calls or sends texts on behalf of your sales team, who uses it, and what kind of message it sends. This exercise often uncovers forgotten tools still connected to old lists.

## Treat "stop" as a company-wide decision

The rule that one telemarketing opt-out covers all future telemarketing from that sender is a reminder that prospects see your company, not your org chart. If someone tells one rep to stop, a colleague's cadence from another tool should not reach them next week.

Centralize opt-outs in one place, ideally your CRM, and sync it with every sending tool. Run a simple test: opt out from a test number through each channel, then check whether every tool picks it up. If one doesn't, fix that before you send anything else.

## Make the exit easy and visible

If you choose to designate a single opt-out method, the disclosure has to be clear. Beyond compliance, that's good sales practice. A prospect who can easily say "not interested" saves your rep from chasing a dead lead, and a prospect who feels trapped is more likely to complain or post a screenshot.

Review your call scripts and text templates. Is the way to opt out obvious? Would a busy operations manager understand it in a few seconds?

## Separate selling from service messages

The distinction between telemarketing and informational messages matters here. A customer who stops promotional texts may still want delivery updates or invoice reminders. Mixing them in the same sequence creates confusion for the customer and risk for you.

Label each message template in your tools as either sales or service. It takes an afternoon and makes future reporting much easier.

## Use opt-outs as a qualification signal

A rising opt-out rate is often the first sign that your list, your timing or your message is off. Look at where opt-outs cluster: a particular industry, a particular template, a particular time of day. That pattern tells you where to tighten your targeting.

For example, a logistics software vendor might notice that most opt-outs come from very small fleets that were never a good fit. Removing them from the list improves results and reduces the number of people annoyed by irrelevant messages.

## Your next step

Before November 25, send yourself an opt-out through each channel your team uses, and see how long it takes to reach every tool. If the answer is "I'm not sure", that's where to start.
