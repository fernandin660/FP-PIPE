Most sales teams try to grow by adding more accounts per rep. One kitchen supplier in the UK just went the other way: fewer square miles per person, more time per customer.

## The case

According to [kbbreview](https://www.kbbreview.com/88697/news/franke-restructures-sales-team-to-increase-customer-focus/), Franke UK has reorganised its field sales team, moving from five sales territories to eight. It hired three new area sales managers, bringing the team to eight, with two national sales managers covering the North and the South. The new hires come from distributors and other businesses in the kitchen and appliance trade.

The stated goal is smaller geographical areas, closer attention to customers, more support for independent kitchen retailers and stronger partnerships. Sales director Chris Gallagher described it as "the most robust 'boots on the ground' team" the company has had.

It's a small piece of trade news, but the logic behind it applies to almost any B2B business that sells through visits, dealers or resellers.

## Lesson 1: territory size decides how often customers hear from you

A rep with too much ground spends the week driving and firefighting. The accounts that shout loudest get visited; the quiet ones, often the ones about to switch supplier, get nothing. Smaller territories don't automatically mean more sales, but they make a regular contact rhythm possible.

A quick test: list every active account in a territory and estimate how many days have passed since the last meaningful contact. If a big share is over 60 days, the territory is probably too large, or the rep has no system for covering it.

## Lesson 2: design territories around customers, not maps

Drawing lines on a map is easy. A better approach starts from data: how many active and potential accounts are in each area, how much they buy, and how long a visit takes. Two territories of the same size can hold very different workloads, a dense city centre versus a rural region, for example.

Before redrawing anything, count three things per area: current customers, qualified prospects not yet buying, and estimated travel time. Balance on workload, not on square miles.

## Lesson 3: hire people who already know the buyer

Franke's new managers come from distributors and other companies in the same trade. That shortens the ramp-up: they understand how independent retailers buy, what worries them and how they compare suppliers.

For your own hiring, industry knowledge often matters more than generic sales experience, especially in technical or relationship-heavy sectors such as building materials, medical equipment or industrial parts. If you can't hire it, build it: give new reps a short guide on how your typical customer makes decisions.

## Lesson 4: independents need a different kind of attention

Big chains have buyers, procurement processes and annual reviews. Independent businesses usually have an owner who decides quickly but has little time and little patience for generic pitches. They value a rep who turns up regularly, knows their shop and helps them sell more, through training, display ideas or fast answers.

If part of your market is independent businesses, plan a light, consistent touch: short visits, useful material, and a follow-up within 48 hours of every conversation.

## Lesson 5: more reps means more need for shared records

Going from five to eight territories also means more handovers: accounts moving between reps, new people inheriting histories they didn't build. Without a shared record of who said what and what was promised, customers end up repeating themselves, and that hurts the very relationship the restructure was meant to improve.

Before any territory change, make sure every account has an up-to-date history: last contact, open quotes, key contacts and next step.

## Your move

Pick your largest territory and check how many accounts haven't been contacted in two months. That number will tell you whether you have a coverage problem worth solving before chasing new leads.
