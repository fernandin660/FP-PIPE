With email, LinkedIn, WhatsApp and ads, knocking on a prospect's door can feel old-fashioned. In practice, if you sell to shops and small businesses, an in-person visit is still one of the most effective ways to start a conversation. The reason is simple: a shop owner gets dozens of messages a day and ignores nearly all of them, but rarely ignores someone polite who walked in, got to the point and respected their time.

What separates door-to-door that works from door-to-door that just wears you out is preparation.

## When it makes sense

Door-to-door pays off most when:

- the customer has an **address open to the public** (shop, clinic, garage, restaurant);
- the person who decides **is usually on site**;
- what you sell is easier to understand **when you show it** (a coffee machine, cleaning equipment, a service with visible results);
- the deal is worth the travel time.

For businesses that don't receive the public, the logic changes: the "door" becomes a phone call to book a meeting, and the visit happens by appointment.

## 1. Choose who to visit before you leave

Walking down the street and going into every door is the fastest way to give up on the method. Build a list first:

- **The right profile**: businesses that have the problem you solve. Someone selling commercial coffee machines looks for busy cafés and bakeries with old equipment; someone selling management software looks for businesses still doing everything on paper.
- **Signs the business is doing well**: good reviews, foot traffic, years in operation. A business that's doing well has money to invest and something to protect.
- **A smart route**: group visits by neighbourhood or street so you have more conversations in less time.

## 2. Know who to talk to

The most common mistake is walking in and asking "can I speak to the manager?". It works much better to arrive knowing the decision-maker's name.

In many countries this is public information: official company registers usually list directors and owners. Prospecting tools cross-check that data with the business address and save hours of research.

Two precautions:

- **check the data is current**: shops change hands and registers can lag behind;
- **respect anyone who has opted out of sales contact**: some registers let a business say so, and that request must be followed.

## 3. Prepare one specific observation

Nobody wants a generic pitch. Before each visit, note down **one concrete thing** about that business:

- "I noticed the breakfast queue reaches the pavement. A second machine could serve people much faster."
- "I saw that orders are still written on paper and carried by hand to the kitchen."

A line like that shows you've done your homework and opens the conversation with their problem, not your product.

## 4. Pick the right time

Visiting a bakery at 7 a.m. or a restaurant at noon wastes the visit. What usually works best:

- **mid-morning and mid-afternoon**, outside the rush;
- quieter weekdays, which vary by industry.

If the owner is busy, don't push: ask when a better time would be and come back.

## 5. Make your approach in under a minute

A simple structure:

1. **Who you are**, in one sentence.
2. **Your specific observation** about their business.
3. **What you propose**, with a concrete result.
4. **A small next step**: show an example on your phone, leave a leaflet, book 15 minutes.

Leave something behind: a card with a QR code, a printed example, a one-page proposal. The conversation ends; the material stays on the counter.

## 6. Door-to-door doesn't end at the door

Most sales don't happen on the first visit. They happen on the follow-up. So **write everything down** as soon as you leave:

- who you spoke to and what was said;
- their level of interest;
- the date you agreed to come back or call.

A simple pipeline does the job: **New → Visited → Interested → Proposal → Won or Lost**, always with a next activity scheduled. Without it, in two weeks nobody remembers who asked you to come back on Friday.

## 7. Combine in-person and digital

Door-to-door becomes much stronger when it works alongside your other channels:

- **before**: a short message or call announcing the visit;
- **after**: an email or WhatsApp summarising the conversation, with the material you promised;
- **in between**: your online presence (website, Instagram, LinkedIn), which the prospect will look up as soon as you leave.

## 8. Look after people's data

Even in B2B sales, contact details belong to people. In Europe and the UK the GDPR applies, and many other countries have similar privacy laws. In practice:

- say where the information came from if asked;
- always offer the option not to be contacted again, and respect the answer;
- keep only what you need for the business conversation.

## So, does it work?

Yes, as long as you treat it as a process rather than luck: a well-chosen list, the decision-maker identified, an observation that shows you know the business, the right timing, a short approach and organised follow-up.
