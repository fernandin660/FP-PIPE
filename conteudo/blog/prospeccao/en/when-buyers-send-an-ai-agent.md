Most sales teams still picture the buyer as a person reading a website, downloading a brochure or replying to an email. A negotiation reported this week between two of the largest companies in the world suggests that, more and more often, the first visitor may be software acting on the buyer's behalf, and that sellers are already arguing over who controls that moment.

## The case

According to an exclusive first published by Fortune on 25 November and summarised by [TechCrunch](https://finance.yahoo.com/technology/ai/articles/amazon-meta-reportedly-talks-lift-141018682.html) and [The Next Web](https://thenextweb.com/news/amazon-and-meta-talks-muse-blockade-ads-fortune), Amazon and Meta are in talks that could let Meta's AI shopping agent, Muse, buy products directly on Amazon. Amazon blocked the agent around two months ago, and users reported errors at checkout. The reports say the companies have met several times, that the open issues are how search results would be shown and how advertising would work, and that any deal would probably need sign-off from both chief executives. Neither company commented, and no outcome or timing has been set.

The Next Web, citing Fortune, explains Amazon's concerns: an outside agent could shop without users seeing Amazon's ads, it could show search results Amazon doesn't trust, and it could weaken Amazon's direct relationship with its customers. Amazon chief executive Andy Jassy has said outside agents are not ruled out, but they would need a "fair exchange of value." Meanwhile, Meta has reportedly signed up retailers such as Walmart, Shopify and Best Buy for Muse.

This is a consumer story, and the talks may go nowhere. But the tension it shows is already arriving in B2B, where purchasing teams are testing AI tools to research suppliers, compare offers and handle routine orders.

## Your first reader may not be human

If a buyer asks an AI tool to shortlist suppliers, that tool reads what is publicly available: your website, your product pages, your documentation, reviews and press mentions. It doesn't see your best salesperson's charm. It sees whether your pricing logic is explained, whether your specifications are clear and whether your delivery terms are stated anywhere.

Take one hour this week to read your own site as if you were a machine with a buyer's checklist. Can it find who you serve, what you sell, minimum order or contract size, lead times, integrations and how to start? A packaging manufacturer that lists materials, sizes and lead times in plain text is easier to shortlist than one that hides everything behind "contact us".

## Know where you want the relationship to live

Amazon's worry, as reported, is not only about one sale. It is about who owns the customer relationship and the data that comes with it. B2B sellers face a smaller version of the same question every time they sell through a marketplace, a distributor or a procurement platform.

That doesn't mean refusing those channels. It means deciding which part of the relationship you must keep: onboarding, technical support, renewals, account reviews. If a buyer's agent places the reorder, make sure a person on your side still owns the quarterly conversation about what the client needs next.

## Make routine buying easy, so people can focus on the rest

Agents are most useful for repetitive purchases: reorders, standard configurations, catalogue items. If your process for a simple reorder still needs three emails and a PDF quote, a buyer with an AI assistant will find it tedious, and a competitor with a clean ordering path will look better.

Map your most common repeat order and remove one step. Then use the time saved for the work no agent can do well: understanding a new project, handling an exception, negotiating a multi-year deal.

## Negotiate access as a partnership, not a fight

The reported talks are about terms of access: what each side gets in exchange. When a large customer asks to connect its procurement system or tool to yours, the same logic applies. Before saying yes or no, list what you need to protect (pricing visibility, service quality, contact with users) and what you can offer (data feeds, catalogue updates, integration support). A clear exchange is easier to agree than a vague "let us in".

## Keep people where trust is decided

An agent can compare prices and specifications. It can't easily judge whether a supplier will pick up the phone when a delivery goes wrong. In B2B, that judgement still comes from relationships, references and track record. Use agents and automation to clear the routine, and invest the time you gain in the moments where a buyer is deciding whether to trust you.

## Your next step

Ask three of your current customers whether anyone on their team uses AI tools to research or compare suppliers. Their answers will tell you how urgently your public information needs to improve, and which parts of the buying journey still depend on a person.
