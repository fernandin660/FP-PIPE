A customer-success software company is rethinking how it charges and who looks after each client. The details say a lot about where B2B selling is heading.

## The case

According to an [Investing.com exclusive](https://investing.com/news/stock-market-news/gainsight-cuts-jobs-as-it-refounds-business-around-ai-agents-5021270) published on 12 November, Gainsight is cutting about 270 roles as it rebuilds its business around AI agents. Chief executive Chuck Ganapathi confirmed the cuts in a LinkedIn post, without giving a number, and said the core business remains highly profitable. Departing staff are being offered financial transition assistance, continued healthcare access and career support.

Two changes in the plan matter most for sales teams. First, the company is moving away from charging per user seat towards pricing tied to the value customers get. Second, every customer will have a single "Outcome Executive": a dedicated person for the largest accounts and an AI agent for smaller ones. Ganapathi summed up the people side as "fewer people in some roles, new skills in others".

Job cuts are hard news for the people affected, and nothing here suggests other companies should copy them. But the way the customer model is being redesigned holds useful lessons for anyone who sells B2B.

## Lesson 1: every account needs one clear owner

Many customers deal with a salesperson before the deal, an onboarding team after it, support when something breaks and someone else at renewal. Each handover risks losing context, and the customer ends up explaining the same thing several times.

Whatever size your team is, make sure each account has one named owner who knows the history and answers for the result. In a small business that may simply mean writing the owner's name on every account record and agreeing that nobody else changes the plan without talking to them.

## Lesson 2: tier your coverage honestly

Gainsight's split, a person for the biggest accounts and automation for the rest, is a version of something every sales team has to decide: you can't give every customer the same attention. The mistake is pretending you can, and ending up giving the large accounts too little and the small ones nothing.

Sort your accounts into two or three tiers by revenue and potential. Decide what each tier gets: quarterly reviews and visits at the top, regular check-ins in the middle, and well-designed automated emails and self-service at the bottom. Then check every quarter whether the tiers still match reality.

## Lesson 3: sell the outcome, not the quantity

Moving from seats to value-based pricing means the vendor has to prove results. Even if your pricing stays the same, your sales conversations can make the same move. Instead of "you get 20 licences", say "this should cut your quote turnaround from three days to one".

That requires better discovery. Ask prospects what they would measure to call the purchase a success, write it down, and come back to it after the sale. A packaging distributor, for example, cares less about how many features a tool has than about fewer late deliveries.

## Lesson 4: automation should free time, not replace care

An AI agent can send reminders, summarise usage and flag risks. It can't sit with an unhappy customer and rebuild trust. Use automation for the routine follow-up that usually slips, such as a check-in 30 days after purchase or a reminder before a contract ends, and keep human time for the conversations that need judgement.

## Lesson 5: expect renewals to start at the first meeting

If you're accountable for outcomes, the renewal conversation starts when the deal closes. Agree on success measures early, review them on a fixed rhythm, and raise problems before the customer does. Accounts rarely leave suddenly. Usually there's a slow decline that nobody owned.

## Your move

Pick your ten most valuable customers. For each one, can you name the single person responsible and the result the customer expects this year? Any gaps in that list are where to start.
